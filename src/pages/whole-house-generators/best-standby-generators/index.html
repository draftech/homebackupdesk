What a publisher can do — without inventing click IDs — is force the class decision: air-cooled automatic in the 9–12 kW band, liquid-cooled automatic at 20 kW and up, or a portable plus a legal inlet. Everything else is enclosure color and whose app pings your phone.

If you came here from a storm panic tab, slow down. A class mistake costs thousands in the wrong direction: either you buy a 10 kW plant that will never start the heat pump, or you pour a liquid-cooled pad for a house that only needed a fridge and a well on lockouts. Quick picks by job

Most 1,500–2,500 sq ft homes that will shed HVAC

Air-cooled standby, roughly 9–12 kW, propane or natural gas, automatic transfer switch. Covers lights, refrigeration, well (if 240 V is in the load calc), and an air handler — not necessarily two outdoor condensers at once. This is the default US suburban plant.

Large homes, heat pumps, or “run it like the grid”

Liquid-cooled, often 20–24 kW and up, sometimes much larger. Quieter at the pad, longer expected duty, much higher install. This is the class people mean when they say they do not want to manage circuits during an ice storm.

Rare outages, willing hands, tighter budget

A portable generator plus an inlet box and interlock (or a listed manual transfer switch). You are the automatic transfer switch. Cheaper hardware, real carbon monoxide work, and no set-and-forget. See standby vs portable.

Fridge, CPAP, and internet — not a pad

Skip this aisle. A LiFePO4 power station or an expandable home battery kit is the product. Featured carts on this site live there, not here.

Air-cooled means the engine dumps heat with fins and a fan, like a lawn-equipment cousin that grew up and got an enclosure. That is why the 9–12 kW class is cheaper, lighter on the pad, and less happy as a week-long full-load machine in August. Liquid-cooled means a radiator loop, the same idea as a car. That class is built for longer duty, lower neighbor drama at the property line, and the kW numbers people quote when they want two condensers and a well without thinking. You pay for that in concrete, gas pipe, and a truck that needs a path.

Do not treat square footage as a kilowatt formula. A tight 1,800 sq ft heat-pump house can need the liquid-cooled class. A leaky 3,000 sq ft gas-furnace house that will lock out the air conditioner can live on air-cooled. The load list in the buying guide is the ranking, not the listing photos. Who each class is for

Who an air-cooled 9–12 kW is for

  • You will accept a smart load-shed module, or you do not run two condensers during an outage.
  • You want automatic start when the grid dies, not a cord in the rain.
  • Your gas meter or propane tank can actually feed the BTU load at the pad.
  • You want the common US install: one pad, one ATS, one annual service, not a miniature power plant.

Who liquid-cooled 20 kW+ is for

  • The house must behave like the utility for days, including heat pumps or multiple compressors.
  • Noise at the property line already has a complaint history, or the lot wants a quieter enclosure class.
  • You have the install budget and a gas service that will not starve a large engine.
  • You will not babysit circuit lockouts during an ice storm.

Who portable-plus-inlet is for

  • Outages are rare, you will actually maintain the unit, and someone on site can start it.
  • You will pay an electrician for an inlet and interlock, not a window cord.
  • You can store gasoline safely or run propane and still accept manual work.

Who should skip standby entirely

  • You mainly want a fridge, CPAP, and internet. That is a station job.
  • You cannot place a unit with the clearances your AHJ and manufacturer both require.
  • You thought “home battery” meant Tesla Powerwall. Different category, out of scope.
  • You will not exercise the engine. Then you paid for a sculpture.

Caveats that kill “whole house” claims

Altitude, locked-rotor amps, exercise, oil

Nameplate kW assumes a defined power factor, temperature, and often sea-level air. Climb a few thousand feet and the same air-cooled unit is a smaller machine. Ask the dealer for the altitude derate before you fall in love with a brochure kilowatt. A 4-ton heat pump’s locked-rotor amps can still brown out a generator that “looks big enough” on running watts. Starting current is a spike, not an average. If the installer cannot talk LRA, soft-start, or load-shed without waving the brochure, get another quote.

Whole-house does not mean unlimited. A managed panel that sheds the range and both condensers is a valid, cheaper design. A plant that claims to run everything and then trips on the well plus the heat pump is a failed spec, not a failed brand. Exercise the unit on the schedule in the manual — typically weekly, under some load — so the engine sees oil pressure and the transfer switch actually transfers. Budget oil, filters, a battery, and a service contract. An engine that sits eleven months is a project, not insurance.

Placement still belongs in “best.” An otherwise correct 12 kW class in a side yard that violates window clearances or a noise ordinance is not the best unit. It is a failed permit. The Home Backup is not your AHJ, gas fitter, or HOA board. Local rules win. If two quotes disagree on class, the useful fight is the load calc and the gas pipe, not whose badge is on the enclosure.

When a station is the better buy

If the honest job is a refrigerator, network, lights, and a medical device, you do not have a standby problem. You have a watt-hour problem.
