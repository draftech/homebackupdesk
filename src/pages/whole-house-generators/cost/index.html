Equipment vs install vs ownership

Three invoices hide inside “what does a whole-house generator cost.” Equipment is the engine, enclosure, and often a matching ATS kit. Install is concrete, electrical, gas, and the inspection dance. Ownership is oil, filters, a starting battery, exercise, and a service contract that does not expire the week after a storm. Shoppers who only Google the unit price are shopping a catalog, not a plant.

A realistic all-in for a straightforward air-cooled automatic install is often $7,000–$15,000. That is the number to hold in your head. Complicated gas, a long trench, a service upgrade, or a liquid-cooled “run everything” job climbs from there — sometimes well past $20,000 when the house is large and the pipe is wrong. If $7k–$15k ends the conversation, you are not a standby customer yet. That is not a moral failure. It is a product fork.

Ownership is the line people forget. Weekly or monthly exercise burns a little fuel and a little neighbor patience. Oil and filters are cheap compared with a seized engine. A starting battery that dies in year four is a Saturday without backup. Dealer contracts in the $150–$400 band are how a lot of owners actually keep the plant alive. DIY is fine if you will do it. An unmaintained $10,000 install is not “paid off.” It is deferred failure.

Who this cost is for

When the cost is justified

  • A finished basement that floods without a sump, or a well that is the only water.
  • Medical devices, mobility needs, or heat in a climate where a two-day outage is a health problem.
  • Outages measured in days, not hours, especially in heating season.
  • You will be away and still need the house to keep itself — automatic is the product.
  • You already priced hotels, spoiled food, and a flooded carpet and the plant still wins over a decade.

Who should not write this check yet

  • Renters, or owners who cannot get a permit or an HOA blessing.
  • Households whose real budget after tax is a station, not a pad.
  • People who will not exercise the unit or who wanted rooftop solar instead.
  • Anyone who has not listed loads. Kilowatts without a list are shopping, not specifying.

The cost is for the house that has to behave like a house when the utility does not. It is not for the person who had one four-hour flicker and wants a status object in the side yard. If your 240 V well is non-negotiable, compare this number to a split-phase battery path as well — different physics, different invoice — starting at battery vs standby and the named Anker SOLIX F3800 kit. Neither is a pad generator. One of them may be the cheaper honest job.

When it is a vanity plant

Vanity is paying liquid-cooled money so the dryer and both condensers stay on during an outage you have once every three years. Vanity is a Wi-Fi module and a color-matched enclosure while the gas meter is still too small. Vanity is “whole house” as a social phrase when a load-shed 10–12 kW air-cooled plant would have covered heat, well, and food. The extra class is real when the heat pump’s locked-rotor amps demand it. It is costume jewelry when you just did not want to turn anything off.

It is also vanity if you will not maintain it. An engine that never sees oil pressure is a sculpture. Neighbors will still hear the exercise if you do run it; they will not hear the week it fails to crank. If your honest outage history is a four-hour event every few years and the fridge can ride a power station, a $10,000 pad is a hobby. Hobbies are allowed. Do not call them insurance.

Rooftop solar plus a wall pack is a third budget we will not sneak into this silo. Tesla Powerwall is out of scope. If an installer is already quoting a roof, you are on the wrong site by design — see about.

What to ask a dealer

  1. Show me the load calc. Running and starting. Which loads are locked out. If they cannot produce one, they are selling a box.
  2. What is the altitude and temperature derate at my address for this class?
  3. Is the ATS service-entrance, load-shed, or a managed panel — and who lists it?
  4. Will the existing gas meter and pipe feed the BTU draw, or is a service upgrade in the bid?
  5. Who pulls the permit, who calls inspection, who comes back if it fails?
  6. What is included: pad, trench, start-up, Wi-Fi, first oil, warranty labor radius?
  7. What does year-two ownership cost if I take the service contract versus DIY?
  8. Where does exhaust point, and what clearances did you measure? Ask them to walk the lot, not the showroom.

Get the answers in writing. A low equipment number with a vague “install TBC” is how $4,000 becomes $14,000. Compare two bids on the same load list, not on whose brochure is glossier. Cheaper paths that are still backup

If the $7k–$15k all-in fence ends the conversation, you still have backup products. They are just not pad plants. A portable power station covers a fridge, network, lights, and many medical devices for hours, with no exhaust. Expandable home batteries add watt-hours and, on some kits, 240 V. Neither replaces a well-plus-heat-pump plant for a week of ice. Both beat a generator you cannot afford to install correctly.
